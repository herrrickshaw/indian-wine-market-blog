India and the European Union concluded a free trade agreement on 27 January 2026, and wine carried one of the deal's most-quoted numbers: a duty cut from 150% down toward 20%. Read only the headline, and it looks like European wine is about to get radically cheaper on Indian shelves. Read the reported structure of the cut, and a different story appears — one where the concession is real, but built with a floor under it that keeps it away from the price band where the overwhelming majority of Indian wine is actually sold.

What "150%" is actually made of

The number quoted everywhere as India's wine tariff is not one duty; it is a stack. Bottled wine imports currently attract a 50% basic customs duty (BCD), a 100% Agriculture Infrastructure and Development Cess (AIDC) layered on top, and a 10% social welfare surcharge applied to the basic-duty component — combining to an effective rate reported at around 150%. That is a central-government, at-the-border number; it says nothing about what a bottle actually costs on a shelf.

Verify before quoting a court-grade figure. The 50/100/10 split above is reported by customs-advisory sources (Nexdigm, IndiaFilings), not read directly off a CBIC notification in this piece's own research. Treat "150%" as the well-attested effective rate and the internal split as reported pending a primary-source check.

The layer the FTA headline leaves out: state excise

Alcohol sits outside GST. Every state sets its own excise duty, VAT, and label-registration fee on top of whatever clears customs, and those layers vary sharply: Maharashtra charges roughly 35% VAT on foreign liquor, Karnataka taxes liquor at around 20% VAT and moved to an alcohol-content-based excise structure in May 2026. A national tariff cut changes the price entering the country; it does not touch this second, state-by-state layer, so the landed-to-shelf price of an imported bottle in Mumbai and in Bengaluru can move by different amounts even under an identical customs cut.

What the India–EU deal reportedly changes

Coverage of the deal (Business Standard, The Drinks Business, Vino Joy News, Sommelier India, all dated around the 27 January 2026 signing) describes a staged reduction, not an immediate one, and it applies differently by price tier:

The bottle-below-€2.5 threshold is the part of the deal that matters most for what an Indian buyer actually sees. Sula Vineyards, India's largest domestic wine producer, said in its own January 2026 investor note that more than 90% of Indian wine sales sit below ₹1,500 MRP — comfortably under a €2.5 CIF import price — and that this segment gets no tariff relief under the deal at all. On Sula's own account, the FTA's duty cut and the market most Indians actually buy in barely overlap.

Two readings of the same company. Sula's investor note frames the deal as low-risk to its business, since its volume base sits in the untouched sub-₹1,500 segment. At least one market-analyst note (Whalesbook) argues the opposite — that the deal pressures Sula's higher-margin premium range specifically, against a backdrop of softer recent results. Both readings can be true at once: limited exposure on volume, real exposure on the margin-richest slice. Neither is this blog's own conclusion; both are cited as what each source actually said.

The market the tariff protects

Nashik Valley, Maharashtra, is reported to account for roughly 80–90% of India's wine production, running through more than 50 wineries. Sula Vineyards, founded in Nashik in 1999, is the largest single producer; its reported share of the domestic market varies by source (estimates run from roughly 50% to as high as 75%, depending on which analysis is cited), and it produced over one million cases in 2025 before acquiring Chandon India's operations in 2026. Karnataka's Nandi Hills belt is the distant second production region. Maharashtra's own Grape Policy gives a 20-year, 100% excise-duty exemption to wine made from Maharashtra-grown grapes — a state-level protection that predates this FTA by years and that the FTA's central-government tariff schedule does not touch.

The precedent this deal is following, and the one it broke from

India had already run this playbook once. The Australia-India Economic Cooperation and Trade Agreement (ECTA, in force 29 December 2022) cut wine duty on bottles above US$5/750ml from 150% to 100% immediately, falling 5 points a year for a decade to 50%; wine above US$15/750ml went from 150% to 75% immediately, falling to 25% over the same period. That deal also carries a most-favoured-nation clause for Australian wine: if India later gives any other country — the EU included — a better wine tariff, Australia is entitled to match it automatically. A cut aimed at European wine may end up extending to Australian wine as a contractual side-effect, not a negotiated one.

The UK-India Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement (signed 24 July 2025), by contrast, cut duty on Scotch whisky and gin from 150% to 75% immediately, tapering toward 40% over ten years — but reportedly gave wine no duty cut at all. Two deals signed roughly six months apart handled the same product category in opposite ways, which is itself worth noting for anyone assuming "FTA" automatically means "wine gets cheaper."

What this piece does and doesn't cover

This is a policy-structure piece, not a price-forecast: it does not model what a specific imported bottle will cost on an Indian shelf in 2027, because that requires the state excise layer (which varies by state and wasn't modelled here) on top of the reported customs schedule (which itself isn't independently confirmed against the primary treaty text). Market-size figures for the domestic industry — commonly quoted as roughly USD 266 million in 2025 rising toward USD 1 billion by the early 2030s — come from paid market-research firms (IMARC Group, cross-checked loosely against a separate Technavio estimate that uses a different methodology and isn't directly comparable); they are cited here as industry-estimate context, not official government statistics, because no RBI, DGFT, or Commerce Ministry sizing figure for the domestic wine market was found in this research.
